How to Scan a QR Code With Your Phone

No extra app required on almost any phone made in the last several years. Here's the fastest way to do it on iPhone and Android, plus what to check before you tap.

QM
The QuickMark Team August 10, 2026 5 min read

If you've ever fumbled through downloading a "QR scanner" app before realizing you didn't need one, you're not alone. Almost every phone made in the last several years can scan a QR code using nothing but the built-in camera app. Here's exactly how, on both major platforms.

On iPhone

  1. Open the standard Camera app — no scanner app required, on iOS 11 and later.
  2. Point it at the QR code and hold steady for a second, keeping the whole code inside the frame.
  3. A yellow notification banner appears at the top or bottom of the screen with a preview of the link or content.
  4. Tap the banner to open it — a website in Safari, a WhatsApp chat, a WiFi network, or whatever the code contains.

If nothing happens, check Settings → Camera and make sure "Scan QR Codes" is switched on — it's enabled by default, but can occasionally get toggled off.

On Android

Most Android phones (Pixel, Samsung, and others running a reasonably recent version of Android) can scan QR codes directly from the Camera app the same way iPhone does — just point and hold, and a notification will appear to open the content.

If your Camera app doesn't detect it automatically:

  1. Open Google Lens (often accessible from a small icon inside the Camera or Google search app).
  2. Point it at the QR code — Lens will detect and decode it, then show a link to open the result.
  3. As a reliable fallback on any Android phone, opening the Google app and tapping the camera/lens icon in the search bar also works.

If it won't scan

  • Make sure the entire code, including its white margin, is inside the frame — a cropped edge is the most common cause of a failed scan.
  • Hold the phone steady for a full second or two rather than moving past it quickly.
  • Check for glare — QR codes printed under glass, laminate, or glossy paper often need a slight angle adjustment to avoid a reflection washing out the contrast.
  • Move a little closer or farther away — very small or very large codes both have a "sweet spot" scanning distance.
  • If the code looks damaged, faded, or low-contrast, it may have been generated with low error correction — see our guide on how QR codes actually work for why that matters.

    • Before you tap the link

    A QR code hides its destination until after you've scanned it, which is exactly why it's worth a quick gut check before tapping through, the same way you'd hesitate before clicking an unfamiliar link in an email:

    • Check the preview URL your phone shows before opening it — both iPhone and Android display the destination link in the notification banner before you tap it.
    • Be more cautious with codes in public, unattended places — a sticker placed over an existing QR code on a parking meter or poster is a real tactic scammers use.
    • Never enter sensitive information (passwords, card details) on a page you reached via QR code unless you recognize and trust the destination.
