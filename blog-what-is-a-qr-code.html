What Is a QR Code and How Does It Work?

A two-dimensional barcode that fits in a thumbnail and holds more data than you'd think. Here's what's actually happening inside those little black-and-white squares.

QM
The QuickMark Team August 5, 2026 7 min read

You've scanned dozens of them — on a restaurant table, a flyer, the back of a cereal box. But look closely at a QR code for more than a second and it stops looking like a barcode and starts looking like static. So what is it actually doing?

A QR code (short for "Quick Response" code) is a two-dimensional barcode. Where a traditional barcode — the kind on a grocery item — only stores information along a single horizontal line, a QR code stores information across a grid, in both directions at once. That's the entire trick, and it's the reason a QR code the size of a postage stamp can hold roughly a hundred times more data than the barcode on a can of soup.

Where QR codes came from

QR codes were invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara, an engineer at the Japanese automotive parts company Denso Wave. The goal wasn't marketing — it was manufacturing. Toyota's supply chain needed a way to track vehicle parts through the assembly process faster than a traditional barcode allowed, and traditional barcodes were running out of room to store the information factories needed.

Denso Wave designed the QR code to be readable at high speed from any angle, which is why it looks the way it does: three bold squares anchor the corners so a scanner can instantly figure out which way is "up," no matter how the code is rotated or tilted. That single design decision is why you can scan a QR code sideways, upside down, or from an angle without it failing.

The anatomy of a QR code

Every QR code, no matter what it encodes, is built from the same handful of structural parts:

  • Finder patterns — the three large squares in three of the four corners. These let a scanner instantly locate and orient the code, even from an angle.
  • Timing patterns — the alternating dashed lines that run between the finder patterns. They tell the scanner exactly how big each individual module (the small black or white squares) is, so it can read the grid accurately even if the printed code is slightly warped or blurry.
  • Quiet zone — the blank white margin surrounding the whole code. It looks like empty space, but scanners rely on it to know where the code starts and ends. Crop it too tight and scans start failing.
  • Data modules — the rest of the grid. This is where your actual content — a link, a phone number, a WiFi password — is mathematically encoded as a pattern of dark and light squares.

How a scan actually works

When your phone's camera points at a QR code, it isn't "reading" it the way you read a page of text. It's running a small, fast piece of computer vision:

  1. The camera looks for the three finder squares to detect that a QR code is even present, and uses their positions to correct for any rotation or skew.
  2. It uses the timing pattern to measure the grid precisely, module by module.
  3. It reads the resulting pattern of dark and light squares and reconstructs the binary data encoded inside.
  4. Built-in error correction data lets the scanner reconstruct the message even if part of the code is dirty, torn, or obscured — QR codes can typically still scan correctly with up to 30% of the code damaged, depending on the error correction level chosen when it was generated.

That error correction step is also what makes it possible to add a logo to the center of a QR code (something you can do right in QuickMark's generator) without breaking the scan — the code has enough redundancy built in to route around the obstruction.

What a QR code can hold

People often assume a QR code always "contains a website," but the code itself doesn't know or care what kind of data it's carrying — it just stores text. What that text represents depends entirely on how it's formatted. A single QR code can encode:

  • A website URL (the most common use)
  • Plain text, like a note or a quote
  • An email address, with a pre-filled subject and message
  • A phone number, ready to dial
  • An SMS message, pre-filled and ready to send
  • A WhatsApp chat, with a pre-filled message
  • WiFi network credentials, so scanning connects a phone automatically
  • A geographic location, which opens directly in a maps app

Why QR codes took over

QR codes existed quietly in Japanese manufacturing for over a decade before most of the world noticed them. What changed wasn't the technology — it was the hardware in people's pockets. Once smartphone cameras could recognize a QR code natively, without a separate scanner app, the friction that had kept QR codes niche disappeared almost overnight. The 2020 shift toward contactless service in restaurants and retail accelerated that adoption curve further, and QR codes have stayed mainstream ever since.

Common misconceptions

"A QR code is just an ugly link" undersells what's actually a small feat of applied mathematics.

A few things worth clearing up:

  • QR codes don't require an internet connection to scan — the phone's camera reads the pattern locally. An internet connection is only needed afterward, if the encoded content happens to be a link.
  • QR codes aren't inherently dangerous — but because you can't read the destination before scanning, it's worth staying cautious about codes from unfamiliar sources, the same way you would with an unfamiliar link. We cover this in more detail in our guide to scanning QR codes safely.
  • Not every QR code updates or tracks you — that depends entirely on whether it's a static or dynamic QR code, a distinction we break down fully in Static vs Dynamic QR Codes.
