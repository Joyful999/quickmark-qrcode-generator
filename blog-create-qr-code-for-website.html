How to Create a QR Code for a Website

Turn any web address into a scannable link in under a minute — plus the settings that decide whether it scans cleanly on a poster or a business card.

QM
The QuickMark Team August 6, 2026 6 min read

This is the single most common reason people reach for a QR code generator: turning a website address into something a phone camera can open in one tap. It's also the easiest type to get right — and the easiest to get subtly wrong in ways that only show up after you've already printed 500 flyers.

Why link to your website with a QR code

A URL is easy to share digitally — you paste it, someone clicks it. But the moment that link needs to travel through the physical world (a poster, a menu, a product box, a storefront window), typing a long web address becomes friction nobody wants to deal with. A QR code collapses that friction to a single motion: point, tap, done.

Step-by-step: generating the code

  1. Open QuickMark's QR code generator — no account or download needed.
  2. Select Website as the content type.
  3. Type or paste your URL. You can leave off "https://" — QuickMark adds it automatically.
  4. Watch the preview update instantly on the right as you type.
  5. Adjust the appearance (covered below), then download as PNG or SVG, or send straight to print.

Settings that actually matter

The generator gives you more control than you might expect, and each setting has a real effect on how reliably your code scans in the real world:

Error correction level

Higher error correction means the code can still scan even if part of it is smudged, torn, or covered by a logo. For most digital use (a website, a slide deck) Medium is plenty. For anything printed and handled — menus, stickers, packaging — bump it to Quartile or High, especially if you're adding a logo to the center.

Size

Bigger isn't just "clearer" — it's also more forgiving of scanning distance. A rough rule of thumb for print: a QR code should be at least a tenth as wide as the expected scanning distance. A code someone will scan from about a meter away (a poster, a storefront window) needs to be roughly 10cm across at minimum.

Quiet-zone margin

Don't crop the white space around the code tightly to "save room" in a design. That margin isn't decorative — scanners use it to detect where the code starts. Removing it is one of the most common reasons a perfectly good QR code stops scanning once it's printed.

Color contrast

QR scanners work by detecting contrast, not specific colors. A dark foreground on a light background works reliably. Light-on-dark, low-contrast color pairings, or busy background art behind the code are the most common reasons a "customized" QR code fails to scan even though it looks fine to a human eye.

  • Foreground significantly darker than background
  • Quiet zone left untouched around all four sides
  • Error correction set to Quartile or High for anything printed
  • Tested on at least two different phones before going to print

    • Test before you print

    Always scan the exact file you're about to print or publish — not just the on-screen preview — from a normal phone, at a normal distance, under normal lighting. It takes fifteen seconds and it's the difference between catching a mistake for free and reprinting five hundred flyers.

    Where to put it

    A website QR code works best somewhere a person is already standing still with a phone in hand: a checkout counter, a table, a waiting room, the back of a business card, a product's packaging. It works worst on anything moving fast — a billboard glimpsed from a car isn't a realistic scanning opportunity.

    Mistakes to avoid

    • Linking to a page that isn't mobile-friendly. Almost every scan happens on a phone — if the destination page is hard to use on a small screen, the QR code did its job and the website let it down.
    • Using a URL that will change later. If you might restructure your website, consider whether you need a dynamic QR code instead, so the destination can be updated without reprinting.
    • Skipping the print test. Screens are far more forgiving than paper — ink bleed, glare, and lamination can all reduce scannability in ways a screen preview won't show you.
